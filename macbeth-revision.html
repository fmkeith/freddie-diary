Summarise the plot of Macbeth in five steps.

Plot
1. Witches prophecy — Macbeth will be king.
2. Macbeth kills Duncan with Lady Macbeth's encouragement.
3. Macbeth becomes king, has Banquo murdered.
4. Lady Macbeth goes mad; Macbeth loses control.
5. Macduff kills Macbeth; Malcolm becomes king.
Why is Banquo significant to the plot?
tap to reveal ▸
Plot
The witches prophesy Banquo's sons will be kings. This makes him a threat — so Macbeth has him murdered. Banquo's ghost then haunts Macbeth at the banquet, representing his guilt and paranoia spiralling out of control.
How does Macbeth change throughout the play?
tap to reveal ▸
Character
He begins as a brave, loyal soldier. Ambition and Lady Macbeth's manipulation lead him to murder. Each crime makes the next easier — he orders murders without her. By the end he is a tyrant, isolated and desensitised to death: "I have supped full with horrors."
How is Lady Macbeth presented? Does she change?
tap to reveal ▸
Character
Initially dominant, ruthless, and manipulative — she calls on spirits to "unsex" her. She plans Duncan's murder and controls Macbeth. But guilt destroys her: she sleepwalks, hallucinates blood, and takes her own life. She is ultimately more fragile than she appears.
What is the role of the Three Witches?
tap to reveal ▸
Character
They represent the supernatural, fate, and temptation. They don't force Macbeth — they trigger his ambition. Their prophecies are technically true but misleading. They raise the question: is Macbeth fated, or does he choose evil?
How does Shakespeare present the theme of ambition?
tap to reveal ▸
Theme
Ambition drives the entire plot. Shakespeare presents it as destructive — Macbeth's "vaulting ambition which o'erleaps itself" leads to tyranny and self-destruction. But Lady Macbeth's ambition is also her downfall. Ambition unchecked by morality is the play's central warning.
How is guilt presented in Macbeth?
tap to reveal ▸
Theme
Guilt manifests physically — Macbeth sees a dagger and Banquo's ghost; Lady Macbeth obsessively washes invisible blood. "Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood clean from my hand?" Shakespeare suggests guilt is inescapable and ultimately fatal.
How does Shakespeare explore the theme of power?
tap to reveal ▸
Theme
Power obtained wrongly corrupts absolutely. Duncan's legitimate power creates loyalty; Macbeth's stolen power creates only fear. Shakespeare uses Malcolm's restoration to suggest true power must be earned, not seized. The divine right of kings is a key Jacobean idea here.
What does "appearance vs reality" mean in Macbeth? Give examples.
tap to reveal ▸
Theme
"Fair is foul and foul is fair" — the witches introduce this from the start. Macbeth appears loyal but plans murder. Lady Macbeth tells him to "look like the innocent flower, but be the serpent under't." Nothing is what it seems.
Why was James I significant to Macbeth?
tap to reveal ▸
Context
Shakespeare wrote Macbeth (c.1606) partly to flatter King James I. James was obsessed with witchcraft (he wrote Daemonologie). He was also descended from Banquo — so the witches' prophecy about Banquo's sons becoming kings is a compliment to James. The play also reflects the 1605 Gunpowder Plot — a direct attack on a king.
What was the "divine right of kings" and how does it feature in Macbeth?
tap to reveal ▸
Context
The idea that God appointed kings — killing one was therefore a sin against God as well as the law. This is why Duncan's murder is presented as so horrific. Macbeth disrupts the natural order ("nature seems dead") and the country suffers until the rightful line is restored.
How were women viewed in Jacobean society? How does this affect Lady Macbeth's character?
tap to reveal ▸
Context
Women were seen as weak, passive, and subordinate. Lady Macbeth rejects this — she asks to be "unsexed" to gain masculine strength. Her power is transgressive and seen as unnatural. Shakespeare may be suggesting women who seek power beyond their role face destruction — she dies while Macbeth fights to the end.
What is a soliloquy and why does Shakespeare use them?
tap to reveal ▸
Language
A speech given alone on stage — it reveals a character's private thoughts. In "Is this a dagger which I see before me", Shakespeare shows Macbeth's hallucinating, guilty mind before the murder. Soliloquies create intimacy — we know more than other characters do.
How does Shakespeare use imagery of blood in the play?
tap to reveal ▸
Language
Blood symbolises both bravery (early — "brave" Macbeth in battle) and guilt (later — "Out, damned spot!"). The progression of blood imagery tracks the moral collapse of both Macbeth and Lady Macbeth. It is inescapable — even the ocean cannot wash it away.
How does Shakespeare use light and darkness imagery?
tap to reveal ▸
Language
Light represents goodness, truth, and the natural order. Darkness represents evil, secrecy, and chaos. Lady Macbeth calls on "thick night" to hide her deeds. Macbeth tells the stars to "hide your fires". Evil thrives in darkness — and when it is exposed to light, it collapses.
What is dramatic irony? Give an example from Macbeth.
tap to reveal ▸
Language
When the audience knows something a character doesn't. Duncan says "He was a gentleman on whom I built an absolute trust" — referring to the traitorous Thane of Cawdor, but he is about to make the same mistake trusting Macbeth. The audience knows Macbeth plans to murder him.
02 · Characters

Know them inside out.

For each character you need: their role, how they change, their key quotes, and what they represent thematically.

Macbeth
Tragic Hero · Protagonist
Brave soldier corrupted by ambition. His fatal flaw is being too easily influenced — by the witches, by Lady Macbeth, by his own imagination. He retains some nobility until the very end when he dies fighting.
"I am in blood stepped in so far that, should I wade no more, returning were as tedious as go o'er."
Lady Macbeth
Antagonist · Foil to Macbeth
Dominant and calculating at first. She suppresses her conscience to pursue power but cannot sustain it — guilt destroys her. She inverts expected gender roles, which Shakespeare presents as unnatural and doomed.
"Out, damned spot! out, I say!"
The Three Witches
Supernatural · Catalyst
Represent fate, the supernatural, and temptation. They don't create Macbeth's ambition — they reveal it. Their prophecies are true but deliberately misleading. They open and close the moral chaos of the play.
"Fair is foul, and foul is fair."
Banquo
Foil to Macbeth · Moral Compass
Also hears the witches' prophecy but resists temptation. He represents the path Macbeth could have taken — noble, loyal, suspicious but not corrupted. His ghost is Macbeth's guilt made visible.
"Thou hast it now: King, Cawdor, Glamis, all, as the weird women promised, and I fear thou play'dst most foully for't."
Duncan
King · Symbol of Legitimate Order
Represents the ideal king — just, generous, and beloved. His murder isn't just personal — it disrupts the divine order of the entire kingdom. His goodness makes Macbeth's crime more heinous.
"He was a gentleman on whom I built an absolute trust."
Macduff
Hero · Avenger
Represents loyalty, justice, and Scotland's restoration. He suspects Macbeth early. The murder of his family — ordered by Macbeth — makes his eventual killing of Macbeth feel like righteous justice, not revenge.
"He has no children... What, all my pretty chickens and their dam at one fell swoop?"
03 · Themes

The big ideas
Shakespeare explores.

In the exam you'll need to write about themes using evidence. For each theme: say what it is, how Shakespeare presents it, and link to quotes and context.

Ambition
The central driving force. Macbeth's ambition is presented as "vaulting" — it overshoots itself. Shakespeare shows ambition without moral restraint leading to tyranny and self-destruction. Lady Macbeth's ambition is equally destructive.
"I have no spur to prick the sides of my intent, but only vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself."
Guilt
Guilt cannot be escaped. Both Macbeth and Lady Macbeth are destroyed by it — through hallucinations, sleeplessness, and eventually death. Shakespeare suggests a guilty conscience is the truest punishment.
"Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood clean from my hand? No, this my hand will rather the multitudinous seas incarnadine."
The Supernatural
Witches, prophecies, ghosts, and visions pervade the play. Shakespeare leaves ambiguous whether these are real or products of guilty imagination — this uncertainty is deliberate. Jacobean audiences believed genuinely in witchcraft.
"Is this a dagger which I see before me, the handle toward my hand?"
Appearance vs Reality
Nothing is what it seems. The witches' prophecies mislead. Macbeth appears loyal. Lady Macbeth appears gracious. Shakespeare uses this theme to suggest that evil masks itself — until it cannot.
"Look like the innocent flower, but be the serpent under't."
Power & Kingship
Legitimate power (Duncan, Malcolm) brings prosperity. Stolen power (Macbeth) brings chaos and suffering. Shakespeare endorses the divine right of kings — a direct compliment to James I — suggesting God-appointed rule is the natural order.
"Gracious England... 'gainst the undivulged pretence I fight of treasonous malice."
Gender & Masculinity
Lady Macbeth subverts gender expectations. Macbeth is manipulated through his sense of masculinity ("Are you a man?"). Shakespeare questions what "masculine" truly means — physical violence or moral strength?
"Unsex me here, and fill me from the crown to the toe top-full of direst cruelty."

Macbeth
Macbeth — Act 1
"Stars, hide your fires; let not light see my black and deep desires."
Said after Duncan announces Malcolm as heir — Macbeth is already thinking of murder. The contrast of light and dark mirrors the theme of appearance vs reality. He wants to hide his true nature even from the natural world. "Black and deep" suggests his desires are both evil and buried.

Macbeth — Act 2
"Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood clean from my hand?"
Immediately after Duncan's murder. The hyperbole of "great Neptune's ocean" shows the scale of his guilt — it is oceanic, uncontainable. Blood symbolises guilt that cannot be removed. Dramatically ironic when later Lady Macbeth obsessively tries to wash blood from her hands.
guiltblood imageryhyperbole
Macbeth — Act 3
"I am in blood stepped in so far that, should I wade no more, returning were as tedious as go o'er."
A key turning point — Macbeth accepts he is beyond redemption. The metaphor of wading through blood shows how he is trapped by his own crimes. "Tedious" is chilling — it shows he now views murder as merely inconvenient rather than horrifying. His moral collapse is complete.
guiltambitionmetaphor
Lady Macbeth
Lady Macbeth — Act 1
"Unsex me here, and fill me from the crown to the toe top-full of direst cruelty."
Lady Macbeth calls on spirits to remove her femininity so she can commit murder. "Unsex" reflects Jacobean belief that women were incapable of extreme violence. She rejects her gender role — which Shakespeare presents as transgressive and ultimately self-destructive. The word "direst" shows the extremity she seeks.
gendersupernaturalpower
Lady Macbeth — Act 5
"Out, damned spot! out, I say!"
Lady Macbeth sleepwalking, trying to wash imaginary blood from her hands. Ironically echoes her earlier confidence — "A little water clears us of this deed." The fragmented syntax and repetition show her mental disintegration. She who suppressed guilt is now consumed by it. The "spot" she cannot remove is her guilt made physical.

The Witches
The Witches — Act 1
"Fair is foul, and foul is fair."
The paradox that opens the play establishes its central theme — nothing is as it appears. Good and evil are inverted. This single line foreshadows every act of deception in the play. The chiasmus (mirror structure) reflects the inversion of the natural order that Macbeth's crimes will cause.

Act 1, Scene 7 — Macbeth alone, before Duncan's murder
"If it were done when 'tis done, then 'twere well
It were done quickly: if the assassination
Could trammel up the consequence, and catch
With his surcease success; that but this blow
Might be the be-all and the end-all here...

I have no spur
To prick the sides of my intent, but only
Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself
And falls on the other."
Q1 · First Impressions
What is Macbeth thinking about in this soliloquy? What does his hesitation suggest about his character?
He's weighing up the murder — what does that tell us about who he still is at this point?
Q2 · Language Analysis
Analyse the metaphor "vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself." What does Shakespeare suggest about ambition here?
Think: what is vaulting? What happens when a vaulter o'erleaps? What does this say about the nature of ambition?
Q3 · Form & Structure
This is a soliloquy. Why does Shakespeare choose this form for this moment? What does the audience learn that no other character knows?
What's the dramatic effect of being alone with Macbeth's thoughts here?
Q4 · Wider Play
How does Shakespeare present ambition elsewhere in the play? Refer to at least one other moment and one quote.
Think about Lady Macbeth, or the later Macbeth who has stopped hesitating entirely.
Act 2, Scene 2 — Immediately after Duncan's murder
Macbeth: "I have done the deed. Didst thou not hear a noise?..."

"Methought I heard a voice cry 'Sleep no more!
Macbeth does murder sleep' — the innocent sleep,
Sleep that knits up the ravelled sleeve of care...
Glamis hath murdered sleep, and therefore Cawdor
Shall sleep no more; Macbeth shall sleep no more."
Q1 · Symbolism
What does "sleep" symbolise in this extract? What does murdering sleep mean for Macbeth?
Sleep = innocence, peace, rest. What has Macbeth lost by committing this murder?
Q2 · Language
Analyse "Sleep that knits up the ravelled sleeve of care." What technique is used and what effect does it create?
It's a metaphor — what does sleep do to worry? What does "ravelled sleeve" suggest?
Q3 · Character
Compare Macbeth's state of mind here with Lady Macbeth's in the same scene. Who seems more in control?
Lady Macbeth says "A little water clears us of this deed." How does this contrast with Macbeth?

Act 1, Scene 5 — Lady Macbeth reads Macbeth's letter, alone
"Come, you spirits
That tend on mortal thoughts, unsex me here,
And fill me from the crown to the toe top-full
Of direst cruelty! make thick my blood;
Stop up the access and passage to remorse,
That no compunctious visitings of nature
Shake my fell purpose, nor keep peace between
The effect and it! Come to my woman's breasts,
And take my milk for gall."
Q1 · Character
What does Lady Macbeth ask for in this speech? What does this reveal about her character at this point?
She's asking to be made cruel. But why does she need to ask? What does that suggest is actually there?
Q2 · Gender & Context
Why does Lady Macbeth ask to be "unsexed"? What does this tell us about Jacobean views of women?
In 1606, what were women expected to be? Why would she need supernatural help to commit murder?
Q3 · Foreshadowing
Lady Macbeth asks spirits to "stop up the access and passage to remorse." How does Act 5 show that they did not succeed?
What happens to Lady Macbeth in Act 5? What does this say about whether guilt can truly be suppressed?
