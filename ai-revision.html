Machine Learning
A type of AI where a system learns from data rather than being explicitly programmed. The more data it sees, the better it gets — like how Spotify learns your taste in music.
What is Natural Language Processing (NLP)?

NLP

AI Bias
When an AI makes unfair decisions because its training data was unrepresentative. Example: facial recognition systems that are less accurate for darker skin tones because training data was mostly lighter-skinned faces.
What is the Turing Test?
tap to reveal ▸
Turing Test
Proposed by Alan Turing in 1950. A human has a text conversation with both a machine and another human — if they can't tell which is which, the machine has "passed." It measures whether AI can convincingly simulate human intelligence.
How is AI used in healthcare?
tap to reveal ▸
Healthcare
Analysing medical scans to detect cancer earlier than doctors can. Drug discovery — simulating how molecules interact. Predicting patient deterioration. AI can process far more data far faster than a human clinician.
What is a neural network?
tap to reveal ▸
Neural Networks
A computer system loosely modelled on the human brain — made up of layers of interconnected nodes. It learns by adjusting the strength of connections based on whether its outputs were right or wrong.
What is automation and how does AI enable it?
tap to reveal ▸
Automation
Replacing human labour with machines or software. AI goes further than traditional automation — it can handle unpredictable situations, make judgements, and improve over time without being reprogrammed.
What ethical questions does AI raise?
tap to reveal ▸
Ethics
Who is responsible when AI makes a harmful decision? Should AI be used in criminal justice? How do we prevent surveillance states using facial recognition? Should AI be allowed to create art or write essays?
02 · Key Terms

Vocabulary to know
for the exam.

These are the terms most likely to come up. Learn the definition, then try to use each word in a sentence of your own.

Artificial Intelligence
Computer systems that can perform tasks requiring human-like intelligence.
Algorithm
A set of step-by-step instructions that a computer follows to solve a problem or complete a task.
Machine Learning
A subset of AI where systems learn and improve from experience without being explicitly programmed.
Training data
The dataset used to teach an AI model. The quality and diversity of this data determines how well the AI performs.
Neural network
A computing system modelled loosely on the human brain, consisting of interconnected layers of nodes.
Natural Language Processing
AI that enables computers to understand, interpret, and generate human language.
Computer Vision
AI that allows computers to interpret and understand visual information from the world.
Automation
Using technology to perform tasks with minimal human input.
AI Bias
Systematic and unfair outcomes in AI systems, usually caused by unrepresentative training data.
Turing Test
A test of a machine's ability to exhibit intelligent behaviour indistinguishable from a human.
Ethical AI
The practice of designing AI systems that are fair, transparent, accountable, and safe.
Deep Learning
A type of machine learning using many-layered neural networks to model complex patterns in data.

"Artificial intelligence represents the most transformative technology of the 21st century. Already, AI-powered diagnostic tools are detecting cancers that human doctors miss. Self-driving vehicles promise to eliminate the 95% of accidents caused by human error. In education, personalised AI tutors can adapt to each student's individual pace and learning style, making high-quality education available to anyone with a smartphone.

The economic benefits are equally compelling. McKinsey estimates AI could add $13 trillion to global GDP by 2030. Far from destroying jobs, history shows that technological revolutions — from the printing press to the internet — ultimately create more employment than they displace. We should embrace AI as a tool that amplifies human capability rather than fearing it as a threat."

— Adapted from a technology industry white paper, 2024
Q1 · Argument
What is the author's main argument? Summarise it in one or two sentences.
Think: what is the overall point they are trying to convince you of?
Q2 · Evidence
What evidence does the author use? How convincing is it?
Look for: statistics, examples, expert opinion, comparisons. Are these backed up? Could they be cherry-picked?
Q3 · Bias & Reliability
The source is a technology industry white paper. How does this affect how much you trust it?
Who wrote it? What do they gain from convincing you? What might they have left out?
Q4 · Counter-argument
What is the strongest argument the author hasn't considered?
What could someone who disagrees say back? What real-world examples challenge the author's view?

"The promises of artificial intelligence conceal a darker reality. Amazon's AI recruitment tool, trained on a decade of hiring data, had to be scrapped because it systematically downgraded CVs from women. Facial recognition systems deployed by police forces misidentify Black individuals at rates up to 34 percentage points higher than white individuals — yet they are being used to make arrest decisions.

Meanwhile, the gig economy — already precarious — faces obliteration. Oxford economists estimate that 47% of US jobs are at high risk of automation within the next two decades. The technology sector's assurances that 'new jobs will emerge' offer cold comfort to a 50-year-old lorry driver. We are sleepwalking into a future where the benefits of AI accrue to a tiny wealthy elite while the costs are borne by everyone else."

— Adapted from a newspaper opinion column, 2024
Q1 · Argument
What is the author's main argument? How does it differ from Text 1?
Compare the overall stance — not just the topic, but the conclusion the author wants you to reach.
Q2 · Language
Find two examples of emotive or persuasive language. What effect do they have on the reader?
Words like "obliteration", "sleepwalking", "cold comfort" — why choose those words instead of neutral ones?
Q3 · Evidence
The author uses specific examples (Amazon, police facial recognition). Does this make the argument stronger? Any limitations?
Specific examples are powerful, but do two examples prove a general rule? Is this selecting only negative cases?
Q4 · Your View
Which text — 1 or 2 — do you find more convincing and why?
You don't have to agree with either fully. You can say "Text 2 is more convincing on X but Text 1 makes a fair point about Y."

"Artificial intelligence is not inherently good or bad — it is a tool, and like all tools its value depends entirely on how it is used and who controls it. An AI system that helps a radiologist catch a tumour two years earlier is saving a life. The same underlying technology, used to build a mass surveillance system, is a threat to civil liberties.

The debate should not be 'is AI good or bad?' but rather 'who benefits, who decides, and who is accountable when it goes wrong?' These are not technical questions — they are political and ethical ones. Young people today will inherit both the opportunities and the problems AI creates. Understanding it is not optional."

— Adapted from an academic journal article on technology ethics, 2025
Q1 · Argument
This text takes a different approach to texts 1 and 2. What is its central claim?
Look at the question it says we should be asking instead. That's the key.
Q2 · Structure
How does the author use contrast (e.g. the radiologist vs surveillance example)?
Why show both a positive and negative use of the same technology? What does this technique achieve?
Q3 · Audience
The final sentence addresses young people directly. Why might the author have done this?
Think about who the intended reader is and how this line is meant to make them feel.
Q4 · Synthesis
Using all three texts, write a short paragraph (4–6 sentences) summing up the debate around AI. Use evidence from at least two texts.
Try: "Texts 1 and 3 agree that... however Text 2 argues... On balance..."
